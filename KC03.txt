Chapter 3

Quantum Processing Unit (QPU)
A Quantum-Inspired Parallel Processing Architecture for QVM

Karel Cápek

Abstract

The Quantum Processing Unit (QPU) is introduced as a massively paral-
lel, quantum-inspired execution substrate designed to accelerate operator-
based computation within the QVM ecosystem. Analogous to the role of
GPUs in classical systems, QPUs execute QFM operators, diffusion flows,
spectral transforms, and structured operator programs at scale—while
remaining fully deterministic, reproducible, and auditable.
Unlike conventional accelerators, QPU execution is intrinsically regulated.
All workloads are authorized, classified, and bounded by the Quantum
Field Processor (QFP), with safety classes, capability certificates, and
execution limits enforced throughout the entire lifecycle. Acceleration is
therefore inseparable from governance: increased throughput does not
permit increased scope, nondeterminism, or bypass of policy constraints.
The paper specifies the QPU architecture, including operator execution
units, instruction set semantics, runtime scheduling, monitoring and
enforcement logic, and interaction with the QVM runtime. Parallelism is
operator-centric rather than data-centric, enabling efficient evaluation of
global arithmetic dynamics while preserving strict semantic invariance
under concurrency.
Within the broader Quansistor Field Computing (QFC) stack, QFM
defines the mathematical language of computation, QFP governs its
legitimacy and safety, and QPU provides regulated high-performance
execution. Together, these components demonstrate that performance,
determinism, and accountability can be co-designed as integral properties
of operator-first computational infrastructure.

1 Introduction
As operator-based computation scales in complexity and scope, the dominant bottleneck shifts
from expressiveness to execution. While Quansistor Field Mathematics (QFM) provides a
mathematically rich language for global, multiplicative operator dynamics, and the Quantum
Field Processor (QFP) defines how such computation may be executed safely and legitimately,
an additional architectural layer is required to make large-scale execution practical.
The Quantum Processing Unit (QPU) is introduced as this layer. Its role is to provide high-
throughput, massively parallel execution of QFM operator programs within the regulatory and

62



safety envelope enforced by QFP.

1.1 Position of QPU Within the QVM Stack

The QPU must be understood in relation to the other components of the QVM ecosystem.

• QFM defines what computation is: an operator-theoretic language over arithmetic Hilbert
spaces.

• QFP defines whether and how computation may execute: safety, permissions, and auditabil-
ity.

• QPU defines how efficiently computation is executed: parallel acceleration, throughput,
and scaling.

This separation of concerns is intentional. QPU does not reinterpret operator semantics, nor
does it weaken regulatory constraints. It exists to accelerate execution that has already been
authorized and classified by QFP.

1.2 Motivation for a Dedicated Processing Unit

Operator-based computation exhibits characteristics that strain classical execution models.
These include:

• global state evolution rather than local register updates,
• non-local operator effects across large index spaces,
• high degrees of implicit parallelism,
• heavy reliance on spectral transforms and diffusion processes.

Executing such workloads on general-purpose processors is possible but inefficient. The QPU
addresses this mismatch by providing execution units designed specifically for operator application,
composition, and propagation.

1.3 Analogy to Classical Accelerators

The role of QPU is analogous to that of a GPU in classical computing, but the analogy is limited.

• GPUs accelerate data-parallel numerical kernels.
• QPUs accelerate operator-parallel arithmetic dynamics.

Unlike GPUs, QPUs do not execute arbitrary programs. They execute a restricted instruction
set derived from QFM operators and governed by QFP constraints. Acceleration is therefore
inseparable from regulation.

1.4 Regulated Acceleration

A central design principle of QPU is that acceleration must not bypass control.
In traditional systems, accelerators are often opaque execution domains with limited visibility or
governance. In contrast, QPU execution is fully integrated into the QFP pipeline:

63



• all QPU workloads originate from authorized execution requests,
• safety classes and capability bounds are enforced at dispatch time,
• execution is monitored and auditable,
• results are sealed and verified in the same manner as non-accelerated execution.

The QPU is therefore not a loophole, but an extension of regulated execution capacity.

1.5 Scope of the QPU Architecture

The QPU architecture defined in this paper is intentionally abstract.
It specifies:

• execution units and their responsibilities,
• an instruction model for operator execution,
• runtime semantics and scheduling principles,
• security and compliance integration.

It does not specify:
• a particular hardware implementation,
• a fixed memory hierarchy,
• a single deployment environment.

This allows QPU to be instantiated as specialized hardware, firmware, or highly optimized
software, without altering its semantics.

1.6 Threat Model and Constraints

Acceleration increases risk as well as capability. QPU is therefore designed under explicit
constraints.

• No execution may exceed bounds authorized by QFP.
• No instruction may induce unclassified operator behavior.
• No execution context may persist beyond its permitted lifetime.

These constraints are architectural, not advisory. Violations result in deterministic termination
and sealed audit records.

1.7 Determinism and Reproducibility

Despite its parallel nature, QPU execution is deterministic by design.
Given:

• the same operator program,
• the same execution bounds,
• the same QPU configuration,

64



the resulting sealed output is identical.
Parallelism affects performance, not semantics. This property is essential for scientific repro-
ducibility, regulatory compliance, and governance.

1.8 Relation to Quantum-Inspired Computation

The QPU is quantum-inspired but not quantum.

• It does not rely on physical qubits.
• It does not perform probabilistic measurement.
• It does not require quantum coherence.

Instead, it accelerates deterministic operator dynamics that exhibit quantum-like structure due
to their mathematical formulation.

1.9 Audience and Intent

This document is written for:
• system architects,
• security and compliance engineers,
• researchers deploying operator-based computation at scale.

It is not a marketing document and does not attempt to justify the usefulness of QPU through
performance claims alone. Its purpose is to define a clear, inspectable architecture for regulated
acceleration.

1.10 Roadmap of This Paper

2 QPU Architecture and Execution Units

The Quantum Processing Unit (QPU) is designed as a parallel execution substrate specialized
for operator-based computation. Its architecture reflects the structure of QFM operators and the
regulatory constraints imposed by QFP, while remaining independent of any specific hardware
implementation.
This section describes the internal architecture of the QPU, its execution units, and the interfaces
through which it interacts with the surrounding QVM stack.

65



2.1 Architectural Overview

At a high level, the QPU consists of the following functional components:

1. Dispatch and Control Interface
2. Operator Execution Units
3. State and Memory Subsystem
4. Scheduling and Synchronization Layer
5. Monitoring and Enforcement Logic

Each component has a strictly defined role. No component independently authorizes execution
or modifies regulatory constraints.

2.2 Dispatch and Control Interface

The Dispatch and Control Interface forms the boundary between QFP and QPU.
Its responsibilities include:

• receiving execution workloads approved by QFP,
• verifying execution bounds and constraints,
• instantiating isolated execution contexts,
• routing operator programs to appropriate execution units.

The interface accepts only fully authorized workloads. It does not perform safety classification or
permission checks itself, but it enforces all constraints encoded in the execution request.

2.3 Execution Contexts

Each QPU workload executes within an explicit execution context.
An execution context defines:

• the operator program to be executed,
• resource and time bounds,
• permitted operator primitives,
• isolation boundaries.

Contexts are non-persistent by default and are destroyed upon completion or termination of
execution.

2.4 Operator Execution Units

The core computational elements of the QPU are the Operator Execution Units (OEUs).
Each OEU is specialized to execute a class of QFM primitives, including:

• forward and backward prime-shift operators,
• self-adjoint generator applications,

66



• diffusion and aggregation operators,
• spectral transforms and reductions.

OEUs operate in parallel across independent regions of the arithmetic state space, subject to
synchronization constraints.

2.5 Parallelism Model

QPU parallelism is operator-centric rather than data-centric.
Parallelism arises from:

• independent prime channels,
• disjoint arithmetic index regions,
• concurrent operator applications.

The architecture does not assume SIMD-style uniformity. Different OEUs may execute different
operator primitives concurrently, provided regulatory constraints are satisfied.

2.6 State and Memory Subsystem

The State and Memory Subsystem manages arithmetic amplitudes and auxiliary execution state.
Its responsibilities include:

• storage of distributed arithmetic state,
• efficient access to multiplicative neighborhoods,
• enforcement of memory isolation between contexts,
• controlled persistence where explicitly permitted.

The memory model is abstract and does not prescribe physical layout. It must, however, preserve
deterministic semantics.

2.7 Scheduling and Synchronization

The Scheduling Layer coordinates execution across OEUs.
Scheduling decisions consider:

• execution priorities encoded in the request,
• safety class–dependent constraints,
• resource availability and contention,
• synchronization barriers required by operator composition.

Synchronization ensures that operator dependencies are respected without introducing nondeter-
ministic behavior.

2.8 Monitoring and Enforcement Logic

QPU includes dedicated logic to monitor execution in real time.

67



This logic:
• tracks resource usage against declared bounds,
• enforces iteration and recursion limits,
• detects violations of execution invariants,
• triggers deterministic termination when necessary.

Monitoring operates independently of operator execution units and cannot be disabled by
workloads.

2.9 Isolation and Fault Containment

Isolation is enforced at multiple levels:
• between execution contexts,
• between operator programs,
• between QPU and external systems.

Faults or violations in one context do not propagate to others. This containment is essential for
safe parallel execution.

2.10 Interaction with QVM Runtime

The QPU does not operate autonomously. It is orchestrated by the QVM Runtime, which:
• allocates QPU resources,
• manages lifecycle of execution contexts,
• coordinates multi-QPU deployments.

QPU exposes a narrow, explicit interface to the runtime, minimizing attack surface and complexity.

2.11 Determinism Under Parallel Execution

Despite concurrency, the QPU architecture preserves determinism.
Determinism is ensured by:

• fixed scheduling policies within execution contexts,
• explicit synchronization points,
• prohibition of race-dependent state updates.

Parallelism affects throughput and latency, not semantic outcomes.

2.12 Extensibility of Execution Units

The architecture permits extension through additional OEUs.
New operator primitives may be supported by introducing new execution unit types, provided
they:

68



• implement formally specified operator semantics,
• integrate with monitoring and enforcement logic,
• remain subject to QFP constraints.

This allows QPU to evolve without compromising safety or correctness.

2.13 Preparation for Instruction Semantics

With the architectural components defined, the next step is to specify how operator programs
are represented and executed at the instruction level.

3 QPU Instruction Set Architecture (ISA)
The Quantum Processing Unit executes operator programs through a restricted and formally
specified Instruction Set Architecture (ISA). The QPU ISA is not a general-purpose instruction
language; it is a domain-specific execution interface designed to realize QFM operator semantics
under the constraints enforced by QFP.
This section defines the principles, structure, and semantics of the QPU ISA.

3.1 Design Goals of the QPU ISA

The ISA is designed to satisfy the following goals:
• faithful realization of QFM operator semantics,
• determinism under parallel execution,
• enforceability of safety and capability constraints,
• auditability of instruction-level behavior.

The ISA intentionally excludes instructions that would enable arbitrary control flow, self-modifying
code, or unbounded recursion.

3.2 Instruction Programs

A QPU instruction program is a finite, ordered sequence of instructions, optionally parameterized
and bounded by explicit execution limits.
Instruction programs are:

• immutable once dispatched,
• executed within a single execution context,
• subject to Safety Kernel classification as a whole.

Individual instructions do not carry independent authority; authority is granted only at the
program level via QFP.

69



3.3 Instruction Categories

The QPU ISA is organized into instruction categories reflecting operator roles.

Operator Application Instructions. These instructions apply QFM operators to arithmetic
state:

• forward prime-shift,
• backward prime-shift,
• self-adjoint generator application,
• diffusion step.

Aggregation and Reduction Instructions. These instructions combine or summarize
arithmetic amplitudes:

• weighted sum,
• norm or energy evaluation,
• projection onto subspaces.

Spectral Instructions. These instructions perform spectral analysis or transformation:
• spectral decomposition primitives,
• filtering and band selection,
• resonance detection.

Control and Synchronization Instructions. These instructions coordinate execution:
• barrier synchronization,
• bounded iteration,
• conditional termination.

3.4 Instruction Format

Each instruction has a fixed, explicit format consisting of:
• opcode identifying the operation,
• operand references,
• parameter fields,
• constraint annotations.

Constraint annotations specify limits such as iteration bounds or resource caps and are verified
at dispatch time.

70



3.5 Operator Binding

Operator instructions reference abstract QFM operators rather than concrete implementations.
Binding occurs at dispatch time:

• operators are resolved to execution unit implementations,
• parameter ranges are validated,
• compatibility with safety class is enforced.

This indirection allows operator semantics to remain stable while execution units evolve.

3.6 Deterministic Semantics

The semantics of each instruction are deterministic.
Given:

• identical input state,
• identical instruction sequence,
• identical execution bounds,

the resulting state and sealed output are identical.
Parallel execution does not introduce race-dependent behavior; instruction ordering and synchro-
nization are explicitly defined.

3.7 Bounded Iteration and Control

Iteration is supported only through bounded constructs.
An iteration instruction must specify:

• maximum iteration count,
• termination condition,
• resource budget.

Unbounded loops, self-referential jumps, or dynamic instruction generation are prohibited by the
ISA.

3.8 Memory and State Access Semantics

Instructions may access arithmetic state only through defined interfaces.
Access rules include:

• explicit declaration of accessed regions,
• no implicit global mutation,
• isolation between execution contexts.

These rules prevent hidden side effects and facilitate audit.

71



3.9 Error and Exception Handling

The ISA defines explicit error conditions.
Errors include:

• violation of execution bounds,
• invalid parameter values,
• synchronization failures.

Errors result in deterministic termination and generation of a sealed termination record.

3.10 Safety and Capability Enforcement at ISA Level

ISA execution is continuously checked against:
• assigned safety class,
• capability certificate constraints,
• policy configuration.

Instructions that would exceed authorized behavior are not executed.

3.11 Auditability of Instruction Execution

Each instruction execution contributes to the execution trace.
The trace records:

• instruction identifiers,
• parameter values,
• execution ordering and synchronization points.

Trace data are summarized and sealed without exposing full internal state.

3.12 ISA Extensibility

The ISA is extensible under governance control.
New instructions may be added provided they:

• correspond to formally defined operator semantics,
• admit static safety analysis,
• integrate with monitoring and enforcement logic.

Backward compatibility is maintained through versioned ISA definitions.

4 Runtime Semantics and Scheduling
The runtime semantics of the Quantum Processing Unit define how instruction programs are
executed over time, how resources are allocated, and how parallelism is coordinated without
compromising determinism, safety, or auditability.
This section specifies the execution lifecycle of QPU workloads, the scheduling model, and the
interaction between runtime control and regulatory constraints.

4.1 Execution Lifecycle

Each QPU instruction program follows a well-defined lifecycle consisting of the following phases:

1. Context Initialization
2. Resource Allocation
3. Instruction Dispatch
4. Parallel Execution
5. Monitoring and Enforcement
6. Termination and Cleanup

Transitions between phases are explicit and irreversible.

4.2 Context Initialization

Upon dispatch from QFP, the QPU runtime creates a new execution context.
Context initialization includes:

• instantiation of an isolated arithmetic state view,
• binding of execution bounds and constraints,
• association with safety class and capability identifiers,
• initialization of runtime metadata.

No instruction execution occurs before context initialization completes successfully.

4.3 Resource Allocation

The runtime allocates resources according to the execution request.
Resources include:

• operator execution units,
• memory and state storage capacity,
• scheduling slots and synchronization primitives.

73



Allocation respects:
• capability-imposed limits,
• safety-class-specific ceilings,
• global system availability.

If allocation cannot be satisfied, execution is rejected prior to instruction dispatch.

4.4 Instruction Dispatch

Instruction dispatch maps the instruction program onto available execution units.
Dispatch is governed by:

• declared instruction dependencies,
• synchronization barriers,
• execution priorities encoded in the request.

Dispatch decisions are deterministic within an execution context.

4.5 Parallel Execution Semantics

Instructions may execute in parallel provided their declared dependencies do not conflict.
Parallel execution respects:

• isolation of arithmetic state regions,
• explicit synchronization points,
• fixed ordering for dependent instructions.

Parallelism is exploited only where it does not affect semantic outcomes.

4.6 Scheduling Model

The QPU scheduling model is constraint-driven rather than opportunistic.
Scheduling considers:

• safety class–dependent scheduling policies,
• fairness across execution contexts,
• avoidance of starvation or priority inversion.

The scheduler does not reorder instructions in a way that would alter deterministic behavior.

4.7 Monitoring During Execution

Runtime monitoring operates continuously during execution.
Monitored properties include:

• resource usage against declared bounds,
• iteration and recursion counters,

74



• compliance with synchronization constraints.
Monitoring logic is independent of instruction execution and cannot be disabled.

4.8 Enforcement and Intervention

If a violation is detected, the runtime intervenes deterministically.
Intervention actions include:

• throttling execution,

• suspending instruction dispatch,

• terminating the execution context.
All interventions are recorded and included in the final sealed result.

4.9 Termination Conditions

Execution terminates under one of the following conditions:
• normal completion of the instruction program,

• satisfaction of a declared termination condition,

• enforcement-triggered termination,

• external revocation of execution authority.
Termination is explicit and results in context teardown.

4.10 Cleanup and State Disposal

Upon termination, the runtime performs cleanup operations:
• release of allocated resources,

• disposal of ephemeral arithmetic state,

• finalization of execution metadata.
Persistent state is retained only if explicitly permitted by policy.

4.11 Determinism Guarantees

The runtime guarantees that:
• execution order within contexts is fixed,

• scheduling decisions are reproducible,

• parallel execution does not introduce nondeterminism.
Performance variability does not affect semantic results.

75



4.12 Interaction with QVM Runtime

The QPU runtime is subordinate to the QVM Runtime.
The QVM Runtime:

• orchestrates multiple QPUs,
• manages cross-QPU scheduling,
• enforces system-wide policies.

The QPU runtime exposes only minimal control interfaces to reduce complexity and attack
surface.

5 Security, Abuse Prevention, and Compliance
Acceleration fundamentally amplifies both capability and risk. The Quantum Processing Unit
therefore incorporates security and compliance mechanisms as integral architectural components
rather than external safeguards.
This section specifies how QPU execution is protected against misuse, how abuse scenarios are
detected and prevented, and how compliance with QFP governance and regulatory requirements
is enforced at the accelerator level.

5.1 Threat Model for Accelerated Execution

The QPU threat model extends beyond classical computation.
Relevant threats include:

• amplification of unsafe operator dynamics,
• circumvention of execution bounds through parallelism,
• covert execution of prohibited operator programs,
• leakage of sensitive arithmetic state through side channels,
• persistence of unauthorized execution contexts.

Security mechanisms are designed to address these threats without reducing the expressive power
of authorized computation.

5.2 Enforcement of Safety Classes at QPU Level

Safety classification performed by QFP remains authoritative during QPU execution.
The QPU enforces safety classes by:

76



• restricting available instruction subsets,
• enforcing stricter resource ceilings for higher classes,
• limiting parallel fan-out and execution depth,
• requiring additional monitoring for high-impact classes.

Safety constraints are applied at dispatch time and continuously enforced during execution.

5.3 Capability-Based Access Control

Every QPU execution context is bound to a capability certificate.
This binding ensures that:

• execution privileges cannot exceed authorized scope,
• acceleration cannot be used to bypass QFP decisions,
• revocation of capability immediately affects execution rights.

Capability enforcement is performed locally by the QPU runtime and does not depend on external
trust during execution.

5.4 Prevention of Acceleration Abuse

Acceleration abuse occurs when computational speed enables behavior that would be impractical
or detectable at lower speeds.
QPU mitigates such abuse by:

• enforcing absolute execution limits independent of speed,
• monitoring cumulative execution impact,
• throttling or terminating suspicious execution patterns.

Faster execution does not permit greater scope.

5.5 Isolation and Side-Channel Resistance

Isolation is critical in a shared accelerator environment.
QPU enforces isolation through:

• per-context state separation,
• restricted memory access patterns,
• elimination of shared mutable execution state.

Where hardware support exists, additional side-channel mitigations may be employed, but the
architecture does not assume their presence.

5.6 Detection of Prohibited Behavior

The QPU runtime actively detects prohibited behavior, including:
• attempts to exceed declared iteration bounds,

77



• unauthorized instruction sequences,
• modification of execution metadata,
• evasion of monitoring hooks.

Detection triggers deterministic intervention and immediate sealing of the incident.

5.7 Integration with Result Sealing

All security-relevant events are included in the sealed result object.
This includes:

• enforcement actions taken,
• violations detected,
• termination reasons and timing.

Sealing ensures that security incidents cannot be hidden or altered after the fact.

5.8 Compliance Enforcement

QPU execution complies with governance and regulatory policy through:
• adherence to policy artifacts defined by governance,
• generation of compliance proofs for each execution,
• retention of audit records suitable for inspection.

Compliance is verified independently of execution performance or outcome.

5.9 Emergency Controls

In coordination with QFP, the QPU supports emergency controls.
These include:

• immediate termination of active contexts,
• suspension of execution classes,
• disabling of specific instruction categories.

Emergency controls are auditable and subject to governance review.

5.10 No Trusted Execution Assumptions

The QPU architecture does not rely on trusted execution environments.
Security is enforced through:

• explicit constraints,
• deterministic monitoring,
• cryptographically verifiable audit.

This avoids reliance on opaque hardware guarantees.

78



5.11 Forensic and Post-Incident Analysis

In the event of security incidents, QPU provides sufficient data for forensic analysis.
Investigators can reconstruct:

• the exact execution context,
• applied safety and capability constraints,
• sequence of enforcement actions.

This reconstruction does not require re-execution.

6 Scaling, Sharding, and Resource Management
The Quantum Processing Unit is designed to operate not only as a standalone accelerator,
but as a scalable component within large, distributed computational systems. Scaling QPU
execution must preserve the same core properties that govern single-unit operation: determinism,
enforceable constraints, and auditability.
This section describes how QPU instances scale horizontally and vertically, how execution is
sharded, and how resources are managed without weakening regulatory guarantees.

6.1 Scaling Objectives

The primary objectives of QPU scaling are:
• increased throughput for operator workloads,
• parallel evaluation of independent arithmetic regions,
• fault isolation and resilience,
• efficient utilization of heterogeneous resources.

Scaling is treated as a performance concern, not as a semantic extension of the computation
model.

6.2 Horizontal and Vertical Scaling

QPU supports both horizontal and vertical scaling.

Vertical Scaling. Vertical scaling increases the capacity of a single QPU instance by adding:
• additional operator execution units,
• larger arithmetic state memory,

79



• higher bandwidth for internal synchronization.
Vertical scaling preserves a single execution context boundary.

Horizontal Scaling. Horizontal scaling deploys multiple QPU instances operating in parallel.
Each instance executes independently under coordination of the QVM Runtime.
Horizontal scaling introduces orchestration challenges addressed through explicit sharding and
scheduling policies.

6.3 Sharding of Execution Workloads

Sharding decomposes execution workloads into disjoint units that may be executed concurrently.
Shards are defined by:

• arithmetic index regions,
• operator subprogram boundaries,
• independent spectral components.

Sharding decisions are made at dispatch time and are immutable during execution.

6.4 Shard Isolation and Safety

Each shard executes within its own isolated execution context.
Isolation ensures that:

• state mutations do not leak across shards,
• faults are contained locally,
• safety constraints are enforced per shard.

Global safety classification applies uniformly to all shards of a single execution request.

6.5 Shard Coordination and Synchronization

Some operator programs require coordination across shards.
Synchronization is achieved through:

• explicit barrier instructions,
• bounded reduction and aggregation phases,
• deterministic ordering of shard-level results.

Implicit or ad-hoc synchronization is prohibited.

6.6 Resource Allocation Model

QPU resource management is capability-driven.
Allocated resources include:

• number of execution units,

80



• memory capacity,
• execution time budgets.

Allocations must satisfy:
• safety-class-specific ceilings,
• capability certificate constraints,
• global system policies.

Over-allocation is not permitted, even under low system load.

6.7 Dynamic Load Management

The QVM Runtime may dynamically adjust resource distribution across QPU instances.
Adjustments include:

• rebalancing workloads between QPUs,
• throttling execution contexts,
• deferring low-priority workloads.

Dynamic adjustments do not alter execution semantics or authorized bounds.

6.8 Prevention of Cross-Shard Abuse

Scaling introduces the risk of abuse through distributed execution.
QPU prevents cross-shard abuse by:

• aggregating resource usage across shards,
• enforcing global execution limits,
• detecting correlated execution patterns.

Acceleration across shards does not permit exceeding authorized impact.

6.9 Fault Tolerance and Degraded Operation

QPU scaling includes explicit fault-handling semantics.
In the presence of faults:

• affected shards are terminated deterministically,
• partial results are sealed and marked incomplete,
• re-execution requires fresh authorization.

There is no silent failover that would bypass regulatory checks.

6.10 Heterogeneous Deployment

The architecture supports heterogeneous QPU deployments.
Different QPU instances may vary in:

81



• performance characteristics,
• supported execution units,
• hardware acceleration features.

Heterogeneity is abstracted by the runtime and does not affect correctness or compliance.

6.11 Accounting and Cost Attribution

Resource usage is explicitly accounted.
Accounting data include:

• per-shard resource consumption,
• execution duration,
• enforcement interventions.

This data supports capacity planning, cost attribution, and governance oversight.

7 Applications and Use Cases

The Quantum Processing Unit is not a general-purpose accelerator. Its value emerges in
application domains where computation is naturally expressed in terms of operator dynamics,
global state evolution, and structured parallelism.
This section surveys representative application classes for which QPU provides meaningful
advantages, while explicitly noting the boundaries of its applicability.

7.1 Mathematical and Number-Theoretic Computation

QPU is particularly well-suited for operator-based mathematical experimentation.
Relevant workloads include:

• large-scale spectral exploration of arithmetic operators,
• simulation of diffusion processes over multiplicative structures,
• evaluation of trace-like invariants and operator statistics,
• parallel experimentation with bounded operator families.

The deterministic semantics of QPU execution ensure reproducibility of results, a critical
requirement in mathematical research.

82



7.2 Scientific Simulation with Global Coupling

Certain scientific models involve global coupling rather than local interaction.
Examples include:

• systems governed by integral or operator equations,
• diffusion or transport phenomena on non-Euclidean index spaces,
• models with long-range arithmetic or spectral interactions.

QPU acceleration enables such simulations to be executed efficiently while remaining bounded
and auditable.

7.3 AI and Algorithmic Search

QPU is not designed to replace conventional AI accelerators, but it may support specific AI-
adjacent workloads.
Applicable cases include:

• structured search over operator-defined state spaces,
• exploration of constrained hypothesis spaces,
• evaluation of global scoring or energy functions.

QPU does not provide probabilistic sampling or learning primitives; its role is confined to
deterministic operator execution.

7.4 Cryptography and Security Analysis

Certain cryptographic and security analyses can be expressed in operator form.
Potential applications include:

• bounded exploration of arithmetic structures,
• analysis of diffusion and propagation properties,
• controlled experimentation with cryptographic primitives.

Regulated execution is particularly important in this domain, where misuse or uncontrolled
experimentation may have external consequences.

7.5 Financial and Economic Modeling

Economic systems often exhibit global dependencies and feedback.
QPU may support:

• operator-based market dynamics models,
• stress testing under bounded scenarios,
• exploration of systemic risk indicators.

Execution constraints and auditability are essential in regulated financial contexts.

83



7.6 Distributed Research Infrastructure

QPU can function as part of a shared research infrastructure.
In such settings:

• multiple users submit bounded operator workloads,

• execution privileges are controlled via capability certificates,

• results are sealed and auditable.
This enables collaborative experimentation without relinquishing control over system-wide risk.

7.7 What QPU Is Not Suitable For

It is equally important to clarify what QPU is not intended to do.
QPU is not:

• a replacement for GPUs in numerical linear algebra,

• a general-purpose CPU,

• a probabilistic or quantum hardware simulator,

• an unrestricted high-performance compute engine.
Using QPU outside its intended domain would yield limited benefit and may introduce unnecessary
complexity.

7.8 Performance Versus Governance Trade-offs

QPU prioritizes bounded execution over maximal raw performance.
In some cases:

• execution may be slower than unconstrained accelerators,

• additional overhead is incurred for monitoring and audit,

• resource limits may restrict exploration depth.
These trade-offs are deliberate and reflect the architectural goals of the QVM ecosystem.

7.9 Early Adoption Scenarios

Early adopters of QPU are likely to include:
• academic research groups,

• regulated research laboratories,

• institutions requiring accountable computation.
In these environments, reproducibility and governance outweigh raw throughput.

8 Governance, Licensing, and Sustainability
The deployment of Quantum Processing Units raises questions that extend beyond architecture
and performance. Because QPU accelerates computation with potentially global impact, its
operation must be governed in a way that preserves safety, accountability, and long-term
sustainability.
This section describes governance models, licensing considerations, and operational sustainability
for QPU-based systems within the QVM ecosystem.

8.1 Separation of Governance and Execution

A core principle inherited from QFP is the strict separation between governance and execution.

• Governance defines policies, permissions, and constraints.
• Execution applies those rules deterministically.

QPU does not interpret policy; it enforces it. Governance decisions are expressed as versioned
artifacts that are cryptographically verifiable and cannot be altered retroactively.

8.2 Governance Scope

Governance mechanisms may regulate:
• which safety classes may be accelerated on QPU,
• which instruction categories are enabled,
• resource ceilings and scaling limits,
• emergency suspension conditions.

Governance mechanisms may not:
• override Safety Kernel decisions for individual executions,
• bypass runtime enforcement,
• modify sealed historical records.

This prevents discretionary intervention in specific computations.

8.3 Capability Issuance and Control

Access to QPU acceleration is mediated through capability certificates defined at the QFP layer.
Capabilities may specify:

85



• permitted safety classes for accelerated execution,
• maximum resource allocation,
• validity period and revocation conditions,
• delegation constraints.

Revocation of a capability immediately affects QPU execution rights without requiring system-
wide shutdown.

8.4 Licensing Models

The QPU architecture itself is neutral with respect to licensing, but its deployment requires clear
licensing frameworks.
Possible models include:

• open research licenses for academic use,
• institutional licenses with regulated access,
• restricted licenses for high-impact or sensitive workloads.

Licensing constraints are enforced operationally through governance policy rather than contractual
trust alone.

8.5 Compliance with Legal and Regulatory Frameworks

QPU-based systems are designed to be compatible with diverse legal and regulatory environments.
Compliance is achieved by:

• encoding regulatory requirements as policy artifacts,
• generating verifiable audit and compliance records,
• supporting minimal-disclosure verification.

Jurisdiction-specific differences are handled through policy variation, not architectural divergence.

8.6 Transparency and Accountability

Accountability is provided through:
• cryptographically sealed execution records,
• verifiable enforcement actions,
• reproducible decision logic.

Transparency concerns system behavior and governance decisions, not necessarily disclosure of
computational content.

8.7 Sustainability of Shared Infrastructure

QPU systems may be operated as shared infrastructure.
Sustainability considerations include:

86



• fair resource allocation across users,
• prevention of monopolization through acceleration,
• long-term maintainability of policy and runtime software.

Capability-based access and explicit accounting support these goals.

8.8 Evolution of Governance

Governance frameworks must evolve as new risks and use cases emerge.
Evolution is supported through:

• versioned policy updates,
• backward-compatible execution semantics,
• explicit transition periods.

Past executions remain verifiable under the policies in effect at their time of execution.

8.9 Avoidance of Centralized Control

Although governance authority exists, QPU systems avoid centralized technical control.
Execution nodes verify policy artifacts independently. This prevents covert policy manipulation
and reduces single points of failure.

8.10 Long-Term Perspective

The long-term viability of QPU depends not only on performance, but on trustworthiness.
By embedding governance, licensing, and accountability into the execution architecture itself, QPU
provides a foundation for sustained, responsible use of accelerated operator-based computation.

8.11 Conclusion

The Quantum Processing Unit completes the QVM stack by providing regulated, deterministic
acceleration of operator programs.
Its architecture demonstrates that performance, safety, and governance need not be mutually
exclusive, but can be co-designed as integral components of a coherent computational system.
